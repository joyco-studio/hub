We built a Monte Carlo simulator and got a result that looked wrong.

The only published comparison we had reported the median share of party hit points lost at each
difficulty: 19% for Easy, 44% for Medium, 67% for Hard, and 71% for Deadly. Our tidy ladder of
goblin encounters came in below those figures at every rung.

The obvious response was to tune the simulator until the medians matched. Increase enemy damage,
lower player damage, adjust healing—whatever made the four numbers line up. That would have felt
like calibration.

It would also have made the model worse.

## The benchmark had a hidden input

The published medians came from 10,000 simulated encounters per difficulty category. The article
documented the party and the rules, but a category such as “Hard” still contains many possible
encounters. Four goblins and seven kobolds can land in the same official band while having very
different hit points, action counts, attack profiles, and time-to-kill.

Our first comparison silently treated encounter composition as irrelevant:

```txt
official band + party = expected outcome
```

But composition was not noise around the result. It was one of the strongest causes of the result:

```txt
official band + party + encounter composition = outcome distribution
```

The benchmark's median therefore described not only its combat model, but also the unpublished mix
of encounters fed into that model. Matching it exactly would mean fitting our simulator to a sample
generator we did not have.

## Sweep the category before tuning the model

Instead of changing constants, we enumerated every plausible single-species encounter a level-one
party could meet. For each creature, we increased its count until the encounter entered a given
official band, ran the simulator, and collected the median damage absorbed by the party.

```ts
for (const creature of lowLevelCreatures) {
  for (let count = 1; count <= 12; count += 1) {
    const encounter = makeEncounter(creature, count)
    const band = officialDifficulty(encounter)
    const result = simulate(encounter, { runs: 600, seed: 0x5eed })

    outcomes[band].push(result.medianDamageTaken)
  }
}
```

That changed the picture completely:

| Band | Compositions | Minimum | Median | Maximum | Published median |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Easy | 75–169 per-band sweep | 0% | 11% | 53% | 19% |
| Medium | 75–169 per-band sweep | 4% | 15% | 58% | 44% |
| Hard | 75–169 per-band sweep | 0% | 26% | 100% | 67% |
| Deadly | 75–169 per-band sweep | 0% | 43% | 114% | 71% |

Damage can exceed 100% because this measures cumulative damage absorbed over the fight. Healing can
refill hit points and let the same starting pool absorb damage again.

The published figure landed inside our range at every band, between our median and 75th percentile.
The simulator was not systematically gentle. The goblin ladder was gentle, and we had mistaken one
composition for the category.

## A label often predicts a distribution, not an outcome

“Hard” is a bucket produced by an XP formula. It is not a promise that every member of the bucket
has the same mortality, duration, or damage profile. The sweep found more than forty percentage
points of outcome spread inside a fixed band. In some bands the spread covered almost the entire
possible range.

This is the general trap: a benchmark category can hide enough internal variation that its aggregate
is mostly a property of the benchmark's sample mix.

- A “mid-range phone” performance score depends on which phones were sampled.
- A “complex page” rendering budget depends on which pages represent the category.
- A “high-risk customer” conversion rate depends on how the risk bucket was populated.
- A model's score for “hard questions” depends on which kinds of hard questions dominate the set.

If your input belongs to a broad category, matching the category average is not automatically
calibration. First ask whether the category is homogeneous enough for an average to mean what you
think it means.

## Split correctness into things you can actually prove

Once the single-median target was rejected, we needed a better answer to “how do we know the
simulator is right?” The replacement was not one looser test. It was several narrower contracts.

### 1. Check mathematical primitives exactly

Dice distributions, hit chances, critical hits, advantage, and death saves have closed-form answers.
Those should be tested against mathematics, not against an end-to-end benchmark:

```ts
expect(hitChance(5, 15)).toBeCloseTo(11 / 20, 10)
expect(meanOf(dicePmf(dice(17, 6, 0)))).toBeCloseTo(59.5, 9)
expect(probabilityAtLeast(dicePmf(dice(2, 6, 0)), 12)).toBeCloseTo(1 / 36, 10)
```

Once the primitives are exact, residual disagreement is confined to visible modeling assumptions:
target choice, action choice, party damage estimates, and the abstractions the simulation deliberately
does not represent.

### 2. Check ordering on controlled ladders

A controlled ladder is still useful. It just answers a different question. Adding more identical
enemies should monotonically increase damage and casualty risk, and higher official bands should not
be safer than lower ones when the composition is held constant.

That validates direction, not absolute calibration.

### 3. Check that external aggregates are bracketed

The published medians remain valuable evidence. The correct assertion is that a broad sweep of the
same regime contains them:

```ts
const outcomes = outcomesFor(band)

expect(outcomes.length).toBeGreaterThan(10)
expect(outcomes[0]).toBeLessThanOrEqual(publishedMedian)
expect(outcomes.at(-1)).toBeGreaterThanOrEqual(publishedMedian)
```

This would still fail if the model were globally too gentle or too brutal. It does not pretend that
we know the benchmark's hidden composition well enough to demand an exact match.

### 4. Expose the largest uncertainty instead of burying it

Party damage was estimated rather than measured and dominated the remaining error. So the product
runs the simulation at the estimate and at ±25%. If casualty risk moves from 22% to 47%, the honest
result is “this depends heavily on your party's damage,” not a confident 34%.

Calibration is not only the process of narrowing uncertainty. It is also the process of naming the
uncertainty that remains.

### 5. Seed every stochastic test

Every simulation run receives a deterministic seed, and the worst observed run records its seed for
replay. Without that, a failed assertion or surprising casualty becomes an anecdote that disappears
on rerun. With it, stochastic behavior becomes ordinary debuggable behavior.

## What would have happened if we matched the median

We would have turned enemy damage up until the gentle goblin ladder resembled a mixed benchmark. The
goblins would look right. Every composition already near the benchmark's upper quartile would become
too lethal. Four green tests would conceal a system-wide bias introduced specifically to satisfy
them.

That is the dangerous kind of overfitting: the test suite rewards it.

The fix is not “never tune to benchmarks.” It is to reproduce the benchmark's sampling process, or,
when that is impossible, weaken the claim to one the evidence supports. We could support ordering,
bracketing, exact primitives, and explicit sensitivity. We could not support “this one composition
must equal that dataset's median.”

The external reference behind this investigation is Tyrel Johnson's
[Calibrating D&D Encounter Difficulties](https://medium.com/@tyrel.j.johnson/calibrating-d-d-encounter-difficulties-528a584e8f39),
which publishes both medians and broad 90% intervals and is candid about the simulation's assumptions.

## The rule

> Before tuning a model to a category aggregate, sweep the variation hidden inside the category. Test
> exact primitives exactly, test controlled scenarios for direction, bracket external aggregates
> across representative inputs, and expose the uncertainty you cannot remove.
